This entry describes the brain MRI cohort of a retrospective observational study at the Mount Sinai Health System in
New York City. The study asked whether a pre-existing non-CNS cancer, or the need for ventilatory support, changes the
neurological symptoms and brain MRI findings of patients hospitalized with COVID-19. Patients were drawn at random
from those admitted between March 2020 and April 2021 who had neurological manifestations and a clinical brain MRI.
The images are not released. The paper's supplement holds only group-level counts.

## Composition

The cohort has 80 adults (44 male, 36 female, mean age 64.5 years, SD 14) with RT-PCR confirmed COVID-19. By design it
has four groups of 20: cancer with ventilatory support, cancer without, no cancer with ventilatory support, and no
cancer without. Ventilatory support covered both intubation and non-invasive ventilation. The cancer groups mixed many
non-CNS tumor types. The most frequent clinical diagnosis was encephalopathy, in 62 patients.

## Acquisition

All scans are routine clinical MRI from several hospitals of the health system, so protocols varied. The typical exam
was at 1.5 T with axial T2-weighted, axial T2 FLAIR, axial gradient echo, sagittal T1-weighted and diffusion-weighted
imaging with ADC maps. MRI took place on average 13.2 days (SD 14.1) after the COVID-19 diagnosis. Scanner vendors
are not reported.

## Annotations

Three fellowship-trained neuroradiologists and a senior resident read the scans in blinded pairs, without knowledge of
cancer or ventilation status, and the three neuroradiologists settled disagreements by consensus. Findings were rated
visually: non-ischemic T2/FLAIR hyperintensities (29 patients), acute or subacute infarcts (21), chronic infarcts
(20), microhemorrhages (19), chronic macrohemorrhages (8), acute macrohemorrhages (6) and encephalitis-like patterns
(6). Twenty-six patients had no relevant finding. Neurological diagnoses were abstracted from the medical record and
confirmed by a neurologist.

## Known limitations

- No images, per-patient reads or derived data are shared, and no terms for them are published.
- The sample is small and has no COVID-negative control group.
- The interval from diagnosis to MRI was long and variable, often because ventilated patients had to be stabilized
  first.
- Exact sequences and field strength varied between hospitals.
